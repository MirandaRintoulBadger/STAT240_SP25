Inference for a Difference in Means

Read the Data

  • This is code taken from the lecture notes which does substantial cleaning of the raw data.
bm_orig = read_table("../../data/TIM.txt",
                col_types = cols(.default = col_double()))

bm = bm_orig %>% 
  filter(!is.na(`K40-Fin`)) %>% 
  filter(Year < 2013) %>% 
  arrange(Year, BibNum) %>% 
  select(-starts_with("Start"), -HalfMar, -Age2014) %>% 
  mutate(Sex = case_when(
    Gender1F2M == 1 ~ "female",
    Gender1F2M == 2 ~ "male")) %>% 
  mutate(Time = pmap_dbl(select(., starts_with("K")), sum)) %>% 
  mutate(age1 = case_when(
    Age < 35 ~ 18,
    Age >= 80 ~ 80,
    TRUE ~ floor(Age/5)*5),
    age2 = case_when(
      Age < 35 ~ 34,
      TRUE ~ age1 + 4),
    Age_Range = case_when(
      age1 == 80 ~ "80 and older",
      TRUE ~ str_c(age1,"-",age2))) %>% 
  select(-Gender1F2M, -age1, -age2) %>% 
  relocate(BibNum, Year, Sex, Age, Age_Range, Time)

Data for comparing 2010 and 2011 women

  • Create a single data frame in a long format with one row per runner for these two years combined.
prob1 = bm %>% 
  filter(Sex == "female" & Age_Range == "18-34" &

Numerical Summaries

prob1_sum = prob1 %>% 
  group_by(Year) %>% 
  summarize(mean = mean(Time),
            sd = sd(Time),
            n = n(),
            se = sd / sqrt(n))

prob1_sum %>% 
  as.data.frame()

  • Sample standard deviations are quite similar

    • About 0.61 minutes (close to 37 seconds) is typical for a sample mean (of over 3500 individual times) to differ from the (unknown) population mean.

CI Calculations Using Tidyverse Commands

### Point estimate = difference in sample means
### Note that diff() calculates x[2] - x[1], so -diff() will show x[1] - x[2]

est = prob1_sum %>% 
  summarize(est = -diff(mean)) %>% 
  pull(est)

est
## [1] 0.2724784
### standard error
  
se = prob1_sum %>% 
  summarize(se = sqrt(sum(se^2))) %>% 
  pull(se)

se
## [1] 0.8613382
### Welch degrees of freedom

df = prob1_sum %>% 
  summarize(df = sum(se^2)^2 / sum(se^4 / (n-1))) %>% 
  pull(df)

df
## [1] 7134.791
### Quantile

t_crit = qt(0.975, df)

t_crit
## [1] 1.960297
#### Confidence Interval
ci = est + c(-1,1) * t_crit * se
ci
## [1] -1.416000  1.960957

CI Using t.test()

  • First, get a base R vector with each sample
times_2010 = prob1 %>% 
  filter(Year == 2010) %>% 
  pull(Time)

times_2011 = prob1 %>% 
  filter(Year == 2011) %>% 
  pull(Time)
  • Then, use t.test().
t.test(times_2010, times_2011)
## 
##  Welch Two Sample t-test
## 
## data:  times_2010 and times_2011
## t = 0.31634, df = 7134.8, p-value = 0.7518
## alternative hypothesis: true difference in means is not equal to 0
## 95 percent confidence interval:
##  -1.416000  1.960957
## sample estimates:
## mean of x mean of y 
##  235.5062  235.2337
  • Note this is much easier than doing the calculation by hand.

Interpretation

We are 95% confident that the true difference in population means for the times of female Boston Marathon finishers (2010 - 2011) is between \(-1.42\) and 1.96 minutes.

  • In other words, it is plausible (at a 95% confidence level) for the population mean from 2010 to be from 1.42 minutes smaller (faster) than to 1.96 minutes greater (slower) than the corresponding 2011 population mean.
  • Note that the value 0 is in the interval, meaning no mean difference between the two years is one of many plausible explanations of the data.

Notes

  • The sample means were 235.51 minutes in 2010 and 235.23 minutes in 2011, a difference of about 0.28 minutes, or about 17 seconds.
    • In a race where the mean finishing time is nearly four hours, a shift of about 17 seconds in the mean finish time is not important.
  • The individual sample standard deviations are 36.2 and 36.6 minutes.
    • It is not at all unusual for individual’s finish times to be more than 70 minutes (roughly 2 standard deviations) from the mean.
  • The large sample sizes (3557 in 2010, 3580 in 2011) mean that the standard errors of the sample means are both about 0.61 minutes, or 37 seconds.
  • The standard error of the difference in sample means is about 0.86 minutes, or nearly 52 seconds.
    • This implies that just by chance, it would not be that unusual for the observed difference in sample mean times to differ from the true population mean difference by nearly a minute.
  • The Welch degrees of freedom, 7134.8, is very close to the sum of the two individual sample degrees of freedom, 7135.
    • This is because the sample sizes and standard deviations are both very close to each other.

Hypothesis Test for \(\mu_1 - \mu_2\)

\(H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2\)
\(H_a: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2\)

Test statistic and null sampling distribution

\[ T = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{ \sqrt{ \frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}} } \sim t_{\text{Welch df}} \]

  • The t test statistic measures the number of standard errors that the difference in sample means is from zero, the null difference in population means.

Calculate.

est
## [1] 0.2724784
se
## [1] 0.8613382
t_stat = (est - 0) / se
t_stat
## [1] 0.3163431
  • In this case, the t test statistic is easily explained by chance as it is only about 0.32 SEs from zero.

p-value

  • Visualize the p-value
gt(7134.8) +
  geom_t_fill(b = -t_stat, df = 7134.8) +
  geom_t_fill(a = t_stat, df = 7134.8)

  • Calculate the p-value
p_value = pt(-t_stat, df) * 2
p_value
## [1] 0.7517514
  • This is not a small p-value. The chance of observing the test statistic or something even more extreme is 75%.
  • Observing something that might happen 75% of the time is not at all unusual and chance alone can explain the discrepency.

Interpretation

There is a lack of evidence to indicate a difference in the population mean finish times between women aged 18–34 in the 2010 and 2011 Boston marathons (\(p = 0.75\), Welch two-independent t test, \(t = 0.32\), \(df = 7134.8\)).
